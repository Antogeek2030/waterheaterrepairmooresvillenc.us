Water heaters work quietly in the background until something goes wrong. Knowing when repair still makes sense—and when replacement is the smarter move—can save money and prevent unexpected cold showers.

## 1. The Unit Is More Than 10–12 Years Old

Most traditional tank water heaters last roughly 8 to 12 years. Once a unit enters that range, the likelihood of additional failures increases. If you are already facing a significant repair on an older tank, comparing the repair cost to a new installation is worthwhile.

## 2. Rust-Colored Water or Visible Corrosion

Discolored hot water can indicate internal tank corrosion. Once the steel tank begins to rust through, patching is rarely a lasting solution. Replacement becomes the practical path.

## 3. Persistent Leaks Around the Tank

A drip from a fitting or valve can often be fixed. Water pooling at the base of the tank itself usually means the tank has failed. Continuing to use a leaking tank risks water damage to floors and nearby materials.

## 4. Rising Energy Bills Without Other Explanation

Sediment buildup and declining efficiency can cause a water heater to use more energy for the same amount of hot water. If bills have climbed and the unit is aging, a newer, better-insulated model may reduce operating costs.

## 5. Frequent Repairs

One repair is normal. Multiple service calls in a short period often signal that the unit is wearing out. At that point, the cumulative cost of repairs can approach or exceed the cost of replacement.

## What to Do Next

If several of these signs apply to your water heater, schedule an inspection. A technician can assess the condition of the tank, anode rod, and other components and help you weigh repair versus replacement based on your specific situation.
