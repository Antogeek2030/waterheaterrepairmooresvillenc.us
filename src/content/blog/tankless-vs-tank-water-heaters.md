Choosing a new water heater often comes down to two main categories: traditional storage tanks and tankless (on-demand) units. Each has strengths depending on household size, usage patterns, and installation conditions.

## How Traditional Tank Water Heaters Work

A tank water heater stores a set volume of hot water—commonly 40 to 50 gallons for many homes—and keeps it ready. When you open a hot-water tap, heated water leaves the top of the tank while cold water enters the bottom to be heated.

**Advantages**
- Lower upfront equipment and installation cost in most cases
- Familiar technology with widely available parts
- Can supply multiple fixtures at once within the tank’s capacity

**Considerations**
- Standby heat loss as the tank maintains temperature
- Finite hot-water supply during high demand
- Larger physical footprint

## How Tankless Water Heaters Work

Tankless units heat water only when a tap is opened. Cold water flows through a heat exchanger and is warmed on the way to the fixture. There is no storage tank.

**Advantages**
- Continuous hot water for as long as the unit’s flow rate allows
- Smaller wall-mounted footprint
- Potentially lower operating costs for households that use less hot water overall

**Considerations**
- Higher initial purchase and installation cost
- May require gas-line upgrades, new venting, or electrical service changes
- Flow rate limits can affect simultaneous use of multiple fixtures
- Periodic descaling is important, especially with hard water

## Which Option Fits Your Home?

Households with moderate, staggered hot-water use often do well with a properly sized tank. Homes that value endless hot water for back-to-back showers or that have limited mechanical-room space may prefer tankless—provided the utility infrastructure can support it.

Climate, fuel type (gas vs. electric), and local water hardness also influence the decision. A brief on-site evaluation is the most reliable way to match a system to your actual needs.
